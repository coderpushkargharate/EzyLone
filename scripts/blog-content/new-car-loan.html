Buying a new car is one of the biggest purchases most families make, and for most buyers it means taking a car loan. The interest rate gets all the attention, but what you actually pay depends just as much on the down payment, the tenure, the processing fee and what is included in the "on-road price".

This guide walks you through how a new car loan works in India, how much you can borrow, how to choose the tenure, which documents lenders ask for, and how to plan an EMI that won't strain your monthly budget.

How a new car loan works

A car loan is a secured loan: the car itself is the security. Until you repay the loan, the lender's name is recorded on the car's registration certificate (RC) as the hypothecation holder. You drive and own the car, but you can't sell it without first clearing the loan.

The usual steps are:

  1. You choose the car and get a proforma invoice or quotation from the dealer.
  2. You apply for the loan with your KYC, income and address documents.
  3. The lender checks your credit score, income and existing EMIs and issues a sanction letter.
  4. You pay the down payment (your share) to the dealer.
  5. The lender pays the loan amount directly to the dealer, and the car is registered with hypothecation in the lender's favour.
  6. You start paying EMIs, usually from the month after disbursal.

How much can you borrow?

Lenders typically finance a large share of the car's price. Some fund up to the full on-road price for strong profiles, while others fund a percentage of the ex-showroom price and expect you to pay the rest. The exact amount depends on:

  • Your income and existing EMIs. Lenders look at how much of your monthly income already goes to EMIs. If a large share is already committed, the eligible amount drops.
  • Your credit score. A strong repayment history usually means a higher loan amount and better terms. See our guide on improving your CIBIL score before a loan.
  • The car model. Some lenders have different funding limits for different models and segments.
  • Your job or business stability. Salaried applicants with a few years in the same job and self-employed applicants with consistent ITRs tend to get better offers.

Ex-showroom vs on-road price

The ex-showroom price is the car's price before registration and insurance. The on-road price adds RTO registration and road tax, insurance, and sometimes accessories and extended warranty. Road tax varies by state and by the car's price, so the same car can have a different on-road price in Odisha than in another state. When comparing loan offers, check which of the two prices the loan amount is based on.

Choosing the right tenure

New car loans are commonly available for one to seven years. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but increases the total interest you pay. Here is an illustrative example for a loan of ₹8,00,000 at 9.5% per year:

5 years (60 months)₹16,801₹2,08,089₹10,08,089
7 years (84 months)₹13,075₹2,98,316₹10,98,316

Stretching the loan from 5 to 7 years saves ₹3,726 a month, but you pay about ₹90,000 more in interest. The rate here is only an example; your actual rate depends on the lender and your profile. Try your own numbers on the EMI calculator.

A practical rule many families follow: keep the car EMI comfortable enough that you can still save every month and handle an emergency. If the 5-year EMI feels tight, consider a slightly lower-priced variant or a bigger down payment before choosing a 7-year loan.

Eligibility: what lenders usually look for

  • Age: usually 21 years or older at application, with the loan ending before an upper age limit set by the lender.
  • Income: a minimum monthly income or annual ITR income, which varies by lender and city.
  • Work history: salaried applicants are usually asked for a minimum time in their current job; self-employed applicants for a minimum time in business.
  • Credit history: a clean record with no recent missed EMIs or settled loans.

Costs beyond the interest rate

  • Processing fee: charged by the lender, often a percentage of the loan amount plus GST.
  • Documentation and stamp duty: as applicable in your state.
  • Prepayment or foreclosure charges: many car loans are fixed-rate, and lenders may charge a fee if you close the loan early. Check this before you sign, especially if you expect a bonus or business income that you'd like to use to close the loan sooner.
  • Bundled insurance or extras: you are not obliged to buy add-ons you don't want. Ask what is optional.

The Key Fact Statement that lenders must give you shows the annual percentage rate (APR), which includes fees. It is the best single number for comparing two offers.

After you finish the loan: remove the hypothecation

When the last EMI is paid, the lender issues a No Objection Certificate (NOC) and Form 35. Submit these to the RTO (online through the Parivahan portal or at the RTO office) to remove the lender's name from the RC, and update the insurance policy as well. Many owners forget this step, and it causes trouble later when they try to sell the car.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get a car loan with no down payment?

Some lenders fund the full on-road price for strong profiles, but it isn't guaranteed. A down payment lowers your EMI and total interest, and improves your chances of approval.

Is a longer tenure always a bad idea?

Not always. It makes the EMI affordable, but costs more in total. If you choose a long tenure, check whether you can prepay later and what that would cost.

Does checking my car loan eligibility hurt my credit score?

Checking your own credit report doesn't. A lender's credit check on a formal application is a "hard enquiry"; several of these in a short period can lower your score slightly. Apply to lenders that suit your profile rather than everywhere at once.
