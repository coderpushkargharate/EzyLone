"Add a co-applicant" and "you will need a guarantor" are two of the most common things borrowers hear when their own profile falls a little short. Both can help you get a loan, or a bigger one at a better rate. But they are not the same thing, and both carry real responsibilities. This guide explains the difference between a co-applicant and a guarantor, how each affects loan eligibility, and what to think about before you sign for someone else.

Co-applicant, co-borrower and guarantor: the difference

  • Co-applicant or co-borrower: applies for the loan with you and is equally responsible for repaying it. Their income can be added to yours to calculate eligibility. The loan appears on both credit reports.
  • Co-owner: shares ownership of the asset, such as a property. For property loans, co-owners usually must be co-applicants.
  • Guarantor: does not borrow, but promises to repay if you do not. The guarantee is recorded, and if you default, the lender can recover from the guarantor.

How a co-applicant increases your eligibility

Lenders calculate how much EMI your income can support (see our eligibility guide for the FOIR method). With a co-applicant, they can use combined income.

For example, if your net income is ₹40,000 and your spouse earns ₹30,000, and the lender allows 50 percent of income for EMIs (an illustrative figure), the EMI capacity rises from ₹20,000 to ₹35,000. That can make a significant difference to the loan amount, especially for long-tenure loans such as a loan against property.

A co-applicant with a strong credit score can also help, but lenders look at both reports. A co-applicant with a poor record can weaken the application.

Who can be a co-applicant?

Lenders usually prefer close family members:

  • Spouse
  • Parents or adult children
  • Siblings, for some loan types
  • Business partners or directors, for business loans

Rules differ by lender and loan type. For property loans, all owners of the property typically need to be co-applicants.

When lenders ask for a guarantor

  • You are a first-time borrower with no credit history
  • Your income is irregular or just below the lender's requirement
  • You are a first-time commercial vehicle buyer; see our commercial vehicle loan guide
  • The loan is for a business with a short track record

Before you become a co-applicant or guarantor

Saying yes to a friend or family member is generous, but understand what you are agreeing to:

  • You are legally liable. If the borrower cannot pay, the lender can ask you to pay the full amount due.
  • Your credit score is at risk. Missed EMIs on a joint loan or a guaranteed loan can lower your score.
  • Your own borrowing capacity may fall. Lenders may count the joint EMI, or the guarantee, when you apply for your own loan.
  • It is hard to withdraw later. You usually cannot exit until the loan is repaid, or the lender agrees to a replacement.

Only sign if you trust the borrower's ability to repay, and if you could manage the EMI yourself in the worst case. Ask to see the loan agreement and the Key Fact Statement.

Tips for borrowers

  • Choose a co-applicant with stable income and a clean credit report
  • Be honest with them about the loan amount, EMI and risks
  • Set up auto-debit so EMIs are never missed
  • Keep your co-applicant or guarantor informed if you face difficulty, and talk to the lender early
  • Ask about releasing the guarantor once you have built a repayment record, if the lender allows it

Tax note for joint property loans

For some property loans, each co-borrower who is also a co-owner may be able to claim tax benefits on their share, depending on the loan type and tax regime. Ask a chartered accountant what applies to you.

Frequently asked questions

Can my co-applicant's income be counted for a personal loan?

Some lenders allow co-applicants on personal loans; many do not. It is more common for car, property and business loans.

Does being a guarantor show up on my credit report?

Guarantees can be reported to credit bureaus, and a default on the guaranteed loan can affect your score.

Can I remove a co-applicant later?

Only with the lender's approval, usually after it re-checks that the remaining borrower can repay alone.
