Three EMIs, two credit card bills and a different due date for each. If that sounds familiar, you are not alone. A debt consolidation loan replaces several expensive debts with one loan, one EMI and, ideally, a lower interest rate. Done right, it lowers your monthly outgo and helps you get out of debt faster. Done wrong, it simply stretches the problem out. This guide shows how to tell the difference.

What is debt consolidation?

You take one new loan, usually a personal loan or a secured loan, and use it to pay off your existing high-cost debts such as credit card balances, small app loans or high-rate personal loans. From then on you repay only the new loan.

A worked example

Say you owe about ₹3.5 lakh across three debts (illustrative figures):

Personal loan A₹1,50,00024% a year24 monthsAbout ₹7,931
App loan B₹1,00,00030% a year18 monthsAbout ₹6,967
Credit card EMI₹1,00,00036% a year12 monthsAbout ₹10,046
Total₹3,50,000About ₹24,944

A single consolidation loan of ₹3.5 lakh at an illustrative 14 percent for 36 months has an EMI of about ₹11,962. Your monthly outgo drops by roughly ₹13,000.

But notice the catch: the new loan runs for 36 months, longer than any of the old ones. Always compare the total interest you will pay, not only the EMI. If you choose a shorter tenure, or keep paying close to your old total EMI, you clear the debt much faster and save more. Try both on the EMI calculator.

Options for consolidating debt

Personal loan

The most common route. No security is needed, and the rate depends on your credit score and income. See our personal loan eligibility guide.

Balance transfer on an existing loan

If one large loan has a high rate, a balance transfer to a cheaper lender, sometimes with a top-up, can do the job. Read about how balance transfers work.

Car loan top-up or loan against car

If you own a car with a good repayment record, a car loan top-up or loan against car may come at a lower rate than an unsecured loan.

Loan against property

For large debts, a loan against property offers lower rates and longer tenures. But your home or shop becomes security, so take this route only if you are confident about repayment.

When consolidation is a good idea

  • The new rate is clearly lower than the weighted average of your current debts
  • You have stable income and can comfortably pay the new EMI
  • You have stopped adding new debt and want a clear plan to become debt-free
  • Fees and foreclosure charges on the old loans do not wipe out the savings

When it is a bad idea

  • You plan to keep using the credit cards you just cleared; this leads to double debt
  • You stretch the tenure so far that total interest goes up
  • You pledge your home for small, short-term debts
  • The real problem is that expenses are higher than income; a loan will not fix that

Step-by-step: how to consolidate

  1. List every debt: lender, outstanding amount, rate, EMI and remaining months.
  2. Get foreclosure figures for each loan, including any charges.
  3. Check your credit report for errors and to know where you stand.
  4. Compare consolidation offers using the APR in the Key Fact Statement.
  5. Pay off the old debts directly as soon as the new loan is disbursed. Do not let the money sit.
  6. Collect closure letters and check that your credit report shows the old loans as closed.
  7. Lower or close unused card limits if you are tempted to spend again, keeping in mind how it affects your credit utilisation.

Effect on your credit score

In the short term, the new application adds a hard enquiry. Over time, consolidation can help: fewer accounts with balances, lower credit card use and a consistent record of on-time EMIs. The key is to never miss the new EMI.

Beware of "debt relief" scams

Be wary of anyone who promises to make your debts disappear, asks for a large upfront fee, or tells you to stop paying your lenders. Stopping payments damages your credit report and can lead to recovery action. If you are struggling, talk to your lenders directly about restructuring options. Read how to spot fake loan agents.
